# Chavalle 2015 — late larval survival need not capture all prior yield costs

Date: 2026-10-08
Study: Chavalle, Censier, San Martin y Gomez & De Proft (2015), *Triticum aestivum × Sitodiplosis mosellana*
DOI: `10.1002/ps.3855`
Status: source-abstract audit; **stage-structure hypothesis / boundary**, not an identified timing-effect size or independent prediction test.

## Why this is relevant

IWE's current effective-stage approach risks treating the abundance of successful late consumer stages as if it were a sufficient predictor of final plant damage. This study suggests an important biological exception: damage incurred before larval mortality, or costs of induced plant defence, can persist even when fewer larvae complete development.

The trials were conducted in infested Belgian winter-wheat fields in 2012 and 2013 with four cultivars differing in earliness and resistance. Adult midge flights overlapped susceptible wheat development, and strategically timed insecticide treatments reduced larval abundance in spikes.

## Source-supported quantitative observations

- Larval counts in ears were reduced **44–96%** by appropriately timed insecticide products.
- In 2013, the highly exposed cultivar Julius gained **1,558 kg ha⁻¹ (18%)** under insecticide treatment.
- In the same year, the midge-resistant cultivar Lear also gained **780 kg ha⁻¹ (8%)**.
- The source reports a **logarithmic**, rather than simply proportional, association between yield and larval numbers.

These observations come from the authors' published abstract. No treatment-level variance, independent field unit table, or pre-harvest egg/defence measurements are currently recovered for reanalysis.

## Competing ecological mechanisms

The authors explicitly offer two explanations for yield loss not reducible to counts of surviving later larvae:

1. Young larvae may inflict damage before dying early in development.
2. The plant's resistance/induced response may itself divert resources or otherwise reduce yield.

These mechanisms are **alternatives, not separately identified causal effects** in the accessible source. The insecticide treatment's yield effect does not isolate either pathway, and we do not infer zero larval load or zero yield damage for resistant plants.

## Why it changes the stage-window prediction

The candidate failure mode is **a persistent impact trace**:

`adult encounter → early larval attack / plant induction → larval death or survival → final grain yield`

The yield response may integrate costs incurred **before** the observed late consumer stage. Hence, a late survival-only predictor can be insufficient even when measured before harvest.

This is a challenge to an oversimplified monotonic idea that the biologically latest measured insect stage is always the best plant-fitness predictor.

The more defensible hypothesis is:

> The effective temporal coordinate for final plant fitness is the stage at which damage or service becomes consequential and potentially irreversible, not necessarily the latest surviving animal stage.

This must be tested: the Chavalle abstract does **not** compare a prespecified adult/host model with a pre-outcome early-damage model and a late-larva-only model on independent held-out fields or years.

## Strict evidence treatment

- Reference class: `seasonal_position_only`; the current receipt does not reconstruct a source-defined quantitative adult window.
- Geometry: `persistent_early_impact_trace`.
- Final endpoint: observed harvested yield.
- Evidence state: `stage_structure_evidence`, never `strong_candidate` or a fitted phase-alignment effect.
- The four cultivars and two years are not treated as independent meta-analysis programmes.
- This programme is distinct from Wu 2015 and Wise 2015, which use different populations, experiments and timing contracts.

## Explicit unlock

Recover the original field/plot table with (a) pre-fate eggs/early attack or defence state, (b) later larval count, (c) insecticide assignment and pre-harvest phenological timing, and (d) final yield per common field unit. If all exist and predictors precede harvest, compare early-impact and larva-only models with cultivar/year-aware validation. Do not declare prospective predictive superiority from the published logarithmic fit or the two yield gains alone.
