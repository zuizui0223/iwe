# Yucca filifera long-form candidate audit

Date: 2026-09-25  
Candidate: `MIX002_YUCCA_FILIFERA`  
Article: Rentería & Cantú (2003), DOI `10.21829/azm.2003.89891776`  
Long-form source: Rentería Arrieta (2000), MSc thesis, Universidad Autónoma de Nuevo León

Decision: **retain P1 `blocked_timing_linkage`; long-form source does not close the missing cohort-to-seed link**

## Why the thesis mattered

The 2003 paper already made this programme unusual among Yucca studies: the same 1998 season contains host reproductive phenology, adult yucca-moth sampling, and a mature-seed endpoint after larval seed consumption.

The main uncertainty was whether the shorter article had simply omitted a fruit-level or cohort-level key that remained available in the underlying MSc thesis.

The full thesis was recovered from the UANL digital repository and audited specifically for that key.

## Host phenology and biological units

The thesis follows 20 tagged *Yucca filifera* plants in 1998.

- 15/20 produced inflorescences.
- The tagged plants produced 47 inflorescences.
- Only 13 of those reached mature infructescence.
- Reproductive stages were followed repeatedly through the flowering/fruiting season.
- The annexes preserve individual-plant/date tables for inflorescence and infructescence occurrence.

The broader reproductive sampling also collected 146 panicles at roughly 10-day intervals from March through August for flower/fruit developmental measurements.

Thus the plant side has substantially finer temporal provenance than is visible in the article.

## Partner-activity window

The thesis also strengthens the partner side.

During flowering in 1998, adult *Tegeticula yuccasella* were sampled nocturnally with UV traps between 5 March and 18 May. The thesis reports 24 nocturnal samples across the two study sites; the later article summarizes 11 sampling dates for the focal presentation.

The biological ordering is clear:

- adult moth density is highest in March, coincident with the main mature-flower period;
- moths occur in March and April;
- no moths are captured in May even though flowers remain.

This is a directly measured same-season partner window. It is not inferred from seed damage.

## Final post-cost reproductive surface

The thesis separately scores mature seed fate in 500 mature fruits.

Reported mature-fruit averages include approximately:

- 64.2 viable mature seeds per fruit;
- 3.8 damaged mature seeds per fruit;
- additional underdeveloped seeds.

The endpoint therefore includes the seed-consumption cost of the nursery pollinator and is biologically appropriate for the mixed class.

## The exact missing link

The long-form thesis does **not** preserve the flowering date/cohort of those 500 mature fruits in the seed-fate table.

This distinction is decisive.

The source contains:

1. individual/date host phenology;
2. a quantitative same-season adult-moth activity curve; and
3. final mature viable/damaged seed counts;

but it does not provide a source-backed mapping:

`mature fruit -> source flowering cohort/date`.

Therefore the following contrast cannot be reconstructed:

`March high-overlap final viable seeds`
versus
`May low/zero-moth-overlap final viable seeds`.

The presence of individual inflorescence and infructescence dates in the annexes does not solve this by itself because the seed-fate sample is not keyed to those individual records.

## What IWE will not do

IWE will not:

- multiply date-specific fruit counts by the pooled mature-seed mean;
- assign the 500 fruits to months from the overall flowering distribution;
- treat pooled seed damage as a month-specific outcome;
- infer the seed sample's flowering cohort from fruit maturity date;
- model a synthetic final-seed surface from separate phenology and pooled-seed tables.

All of those routes would manufacture provenance absent from the source.

## Unlock condition

The candidate becomes quantitatively actionable only if one of the following is recovered:

- raw fruit records containing plant/inflorescence identity and flowering date;
- a source table reporting final viable/damaged seeds by flowering month/cohort;
- another publication from the same 1998 programme that explicitly links mature seed fate to the recorded reproductive cohort.

Until then the candidate remains P1 because it passes the biological timing and final-fitness gates, but it does not satisfy the effect-construction gate at the correct time grain.

## Independent Yucca programme screen: Dodd & Linhart 1994

Dodd & Linhart (1994), DOI `10.2307/2445762`, is biologically rich but does not solve this problem.

That Colorado programme compares plains and foothill/elevational populations. Moth abundance, fruit set and surviving seeds differ among locations/elevations, but the exposure is spatial partner abundance and environment rather than within-season plant–moth phenological overlap.

The linked Dodd (1989) MSc thesis is 77 pages, but the programme's published estimand remains elevational reproductive ecology.

Decision: `rejected_timing`; do not relabel low moth abundance at high elevation as phenological mismatch.

## Next Yucca programme to inspect

Hurlburt (2004), *Persistence of the moth-yucca mutualism at the northern edge of range*, is a more relevant long-form target than Dodd because its explicit problem is the unpredictability of **moth emergence versus Yucca flowering** at the northern range edge.

It should be promoted only if the same year/population units provide final plant seed reproduction with recoverable variance and the emergence–flowering relation can be oriented as a strict synchrony exposure.
